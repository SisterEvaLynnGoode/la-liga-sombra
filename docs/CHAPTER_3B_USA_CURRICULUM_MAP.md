## 1. The thesis

The United States is the **second-largest Spanish-speaking country in the world** by number of
speakers, behind only Mexico. But that fact is not the point of this arc.

The point is that this culture was **invented here**. The jibarito was created in Chicago in
1996 and is served nowhere in Puerto Rico. Conjunto's accordion arrived with German and Czech
settlers in South Texas. The lowrider, Nuyorican poetry, Tex-Mex, Chicano muralism, Spanglish —
none of it exists "back home." It is new, it is American, and it is Spanish.

Every caso in this arc proves that a thing was **made here**, which is also why the arc ends on
the impersonal and passive `se`: *aquí se inventó el jibarito.* That is the grammar of
collective creation, and it is the thesis stated in a verb form.

## 2. The antagonist — **La Curadora**

She does not smash and grab. She **rescues**.

La Curadora removes objects into a private collection because she has decided they are not real
Spanish culture — a hybrid, a dilution, a curiosity — and belong catalogued rather than used.
She takes the object *and* its story. A mural in a vault is a painting; a mural on a wall is a
neighbourhood. Her crime is **decontextualisation**, and every caso is an act of putting
something back where it means something.

She inherits El Cronista's catalogue after his arrest, which is how she knows where everything
is. She is his sibling by design: he had unnaturally **clean hands**, she wears white
**curator's gloves**.

### How students catch her — and this is the centre of the arc

Her Spanish is flawless and completely sterile. **No region at all.** No Caribbean aspirated
*s*, no Mexican diminutives, no Caló, no *asere*, no *órale*, no Spanglish, no code-switching.
She learned Spanish from a textbook and believes that makes her correct.

**The tell is the absence of an accent.** Every genuine witness in this arc sounds like
somewhere specific. She sounds like nowhere.

This deliberately inverts the shame many heritage speakers carry. In this arc the "perfect"
Spanish belongs to the villain, and the way the students' own families actually speak is the
evidence that convicts her. That is the single most important design decision in Arc B and it
must survive contact with the content: **never write a witness's regional speech as an error to
be corrected.**

### Cultural detail to build each caso on

**27 · Los Ángeles.** In 1932 David Alfaro Siqueiros painted *América Tropical* on Olvera
Street. Within a couple of years it was **whitewashed** for its politics and stayed hidden for
roughly seventy years, until conservators stabilised it and it reopened to the public in 2012.
A mural erased by people who found it inconvenient, and recovered. That is this whole arc on one
wall, and it is the case. Around it: Estrada Courts murals, Self Help Graphics printmaking,
lowriders as moving sculpture (*bajito y suavecito*), cruising Whittier Blvd, and **Caló**, the
pachuco slang that gave everyone *órale*, *carnal* and *firme*.

**28 · Nueva York.** El Barrio (East Harlem) and **Loisaida** — the Lower East Side said in
Spanish. The **Nuyorican Poets Café** (founded 1973) and Pedro Pietri's *Puerto Rican Obituary*.
Salsa as a New York invention: Fania, Héctor Lavoe, Willie Colón, Tito Puente at the Palladium.
**Bomba y plena**, Afro-Puerto Rican drum traditions kept alive on concrete. **Casitas** —
little wooden houses in rural Puerto Rican style, hand-built on vacant lots as community space.
This is where **Spanglish became literature** rather than an accident.

**29 · San Antonio.** Tejanos predate Texas; the border moved, not the families. The fact the
caso turns on: **conjunto** is accordion plus bajo sexto, and the accordion arrived with
**German and Czech** settlers in South Texas in the 1800s. Mexican-Texan musicians took a German
instrument and made the most Texan music there is — Narciso Martínez, "El Huracán del Valle";
Flaco Jiménez. And **Selena**, the biggest star Tejano ever had, who grew up speaking English
and learned her lyrics phonetically. For any student who thinks their Spanish does not count,
Selena is the answer. Plus the Spanish missions (UNESCO), Market Square, the breakfast taco.

**30 · Miami.** **Calle Ocho.** The **ventanita** — the walk-up window where you order a
*colada* and share it out in thimble cups with strangers, which is the most teachable social
ritual in the arc. Domino Park. Versailles as the neighbourhood's town square. Celia Cruz and
*¡Azúcar!* The emotional register is **exile**: 1959, Operation Pedro Pan, Mariel in 1980, the
balseros — a culture built by people who expected to go back and did not. Teach honestly that
**Miami is no longer only Cuban**: Venezuelans in Doral, Colombians, Nicaraguans, Hondurans.

**31 · Nuevo México.** The oldest and least taught. **Santa Fe was founded in 1610**, before
Plymouth; these families have spoken Spanish in place for four centuries, and **Hispano** is its
own identity, not Chicano and not Mexican-American. **New Mexican Spanish preserves 16th- and
17th-century forms** — *truje* for *traje*, *asina* for *así*, *muncho* for *mucho* — alongside
Nahuatl, Tewa and English borrowings, and it is genuinely endangered. Plus **santeros** carving
bultos and painting retablos, El Santuario de Chimayó, adobe and vigas, red-or-green chile
(*Christmas* for both).

**32 · Chicago.** The finale, because it is where the two largest US Latino communities live
side by side. **Pilsen** and **La Villita** on the Mexican side: the 26th Street arch, the murals,
the National Museum of Mexican Art. **Humboldt Park** on the Puerto Rican side, where two
45-ton steel Puerto Rican flags arch over Division Street — the **Paseo Boricua**, the only
monument of its kind in the country. And the **jibarito**: plantains instead of bread, invented
in Chicago in 1996, served nowhere in Puerto Rico. The thesis, in a sandwich.

## 4. New mechanic — **el acento**

A short listening stage, unique to this arc and unlocked at Caso 27.

Three or four voices say roughly the same thing. The student identifies **where each speaker is
from**. One speaker has no regional features at all. That one is La Curadora.

### The discriminator is lexical, not phonetic — and that was a forced call

The original design asked students to hear an aspirated Caribbean *s* against a Chicano
diminutive. **We cannot produce that.** The project's ElevenLabs key resolves to only two
distinct custom voices, and while premade library voices do still synthesize (verified: four
tested, all returned audio), they are English-centred voices speaking Spanish through the
multilingual model. They will not reliably produce authentic Cuban, Chicano or New Mexican
phonetics, and shipping a stage that claims they do would teach students to "hear" an accent
that is not in the recording.

So the tell is **what the speaker says**, not how it sounds:

| Region | Lexical / grammatical tell |
|---|---|
| Caribbean (Nuyorican, Cubano) | *asere*, *¿qué bolá?*, *guagua* for bus, *chévere* |
| Mexicano / Chicano | *órale*, *carnal*, *ándale*, diminutives (*ahorita*, *poquito*) |
| Nuyorican specifically | code-switching mid-sentence, carried without apology |
| Nuevomexicano | the archaisms: *truje*, *asina*, *muncho* |
| Tejano | ranch and border vocabulary, *bajo sexto*, *conjunto* |
| **La Curadora** | **none of the above.** Textbook Spanish, no region, no borrowing. |

This is better pedagogy than the phonetic version anyway. It is teachable, it is printable on a
worksheet, it is gradeable, and it does not demand a trained ear from a second-year student.
Distinct voices are still cast per speaker so the scene reads as several different people, but
the puzzle is solvable from the words alone.

Why it is worth building:

- It is the only stage in the game where the student judges **who is speaking** rather than what
  was said, and it teaches dialect awareness, which is a real ACTFL cultural competency.
- It makes the arc's argument structurally instead of just saying it. The student learns that
  regional speech is information, not error.
- It is the mechanism of the finale: the boss is beaten by hearing that she sounds like nowhere.

Build it on the existing `swipeSort` shell if the component allows (sort voices into regions);
otherwise it is a close cousin of `listeningComp` with a speaker-attribution answer instead of a
comprehension answer. **Voice casting matters here** and the clips must be bought before the
ElevenLabs subscription lapses — see §6.

## 5. Guardrails — non-negotiable

These are living communities and some students in the room are from them.

- **No immigration status or enforcement content anywhere.** Not a puzzle, not a plot point, not
  set dressing. This is a game played in a classroom.
- **Real people are never suspects.** Selena, Dolores Huerta, Celia Cruz, Tito Puente and the
  rest appear as culture and as heroes. Every suspect and every witness is fictional.
- **Spanglish and code-switching are shown as skill, never as error.** The villain's monolithic
  textbook Spanish is the deviation from the norm, not the standard.
- Historical injustice appears only where it is load-bearing — the whitewashing of *América
  Tropical* is the actual case in Caso 27 — and never as a mechanic. No trauma as gameplay.
- **Chimayó** is handled as craft and cultural heritage, respectfully. The santuario is not a
  puzzle box and the holy dirt is not a collectible.
- Each caso carries a **"tu comunidad"** prompt so heritage students contribute expertise rather
  than being studied. In this arc they are the experts and should be told so.
